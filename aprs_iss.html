Step zero, every time

Check the status first

The ISS digipeater is not always on, and it has moved between bands over the years. On 4 November 2026, ARISS listed APRS as active on 437.825 MHz (UHF) from the Service Module radio, callsign RS0ISS, still in testing. For most of its history the digipeater has used 145.825 MHz (VHF), the worldwide satellite APRS channel.

Radios on the ISS are also switched off for spacewalks, dockings and some school contacts. A radio on the wrong band hears nothing at all, so check before every session.

How it works

APRS (Automatic Packet Reporting System, created by Bob Bruninga, WB4APR) sends tiny digital messages over ordinary FM radio: your position, a status line or a short note. Each message is one AX.25 packet, about a second of buzzing tones at 1200 bit/s.

A digipeater is a station that hears a packet, stores it for a moment and sends it again on the same frequency. On the ground, digipeaters sit on hilltops and towers. The ISS carries one about 400 km up, so a single repeat can link stations roughly 4,500 km apart.

Passes: when you can reach the ISS

    
You can only use the digipeater while the ISS is above your horizon. That window is a pass, and three moments in it have names:

    

      
AOS
Acquisition of signal: the ISS rises above your horizon.

      
TCA
Time of closest approach: the highest point of the pass, strongest signals.

      
LOS
Loss of signal: the ISS sets below your horizon.

    

    
    
      
  • A pass lasts up to about 10 minutes, horizon to horizon.
    • 
      
  • One orbit takes about 93 minutes, so passes come in runs of consecutive orbits, usually two runs a day about 12 hours apart.
    • 
      
  • Near the equator you get fewer usable passes than at higher latitudes. Prefer passes that climb above 30°: they are longer and stronger.
    •

For each pass, write down the AOS time and compass direction, the highest elevation and its time, and the LOS time and direction. Refresh the app’s orbital data (TLEs) first: the ISS orbit is boosted from time to time.

  






  

    
Doppler: why your frequency has to move

    
The ISS travels at about 7.7 km/s. While it comes toward you, everything it sends arrives a little higher in frequency, and while it moves away, a little lower, like a passing siren. The same happens to what you send up to it.

    
On 145.825 MHz the shift is at most about ±3.5 kHz, which most FM packet radios tolerate. On 437.825 MHz it is about ±10.5 kHz, too much to ignore, so you step through memory channels during the pass. Try it below: drag the time slider through a pass and watch which channel you should be on.

Step-by-step procedure

    

      

      0 of 0 done
      
    

    
Ticks are saved in this browser only, so you can keep this page open in the field.


    

      
OnceSet up your station

      
    
        
  1. In India, WPC rules list amateur-satellite operation under the General Grade licence; Restricted Grade holders should check their licence terms first. Elsewhere, a licence with 2 m or 70 cm privileges usually covers it.
    2. 
        
  2. Easiest: a handheld with APRS built in (Kenwood TH-D74 or TH-D75, Yaesu FT5DR). Lowest cost: any dual-band FM handheld plus an Android phone running APRSdroid, with an audio cable or a Bluetooth TNC. Home station: a radio, a sound-card interface, Direwolf as the software TNC, and UISS or YAAC.
    3. 
        
  3. -7 is the usual SSID for a handheld and -9 for a car. Use your own callsign; the ones on this page are placeholders.
    4. 
        
  4. The setting is called path, digi path or UNPROTO path depending on the software. Remove WIDE1-1,WIDE2-1. On a classic hardware TNC the command is UNPROTO APRS VIA ARISS.
    5. 
        
  5. No GPS needed: type your coordinates, or build them with the packet builder below.
    6. 
        
  6. You will send by hand during passes. Switch off SmartBeaconing and any GPS-triggered beacons too.
    7. 
        
  7. The modem needs to hear weak packets, and power-saving mode can chop off the start of a packet.
    8. 
        
  8. UHF: U1 to U5. VHF: V1 to V3, or simply 145.825. See the tables in the Doppler section.
    9. 
        
  9. On your local APRS frequency (144.390 MHz in India) with path WIDE1-1,WIDE2-1, send a beacon and check it appears on aprs.fi through a local IGate, or that a nearby friend decodes it. Then set the path back to ARISS.
    10. 
        
  10. Heavens-Above, N2YO, Gpredict, Look4Sat or ISS Detector. Update its orbital data.
    11.

  1. Then pick the matching memory channels (U or V).
    2. 
        
  2. The AMSAT status page and the SatNOGS digipeater dashboard show whether people have used the digipeater in the last day.
    3. 
        
  3. Note the AOS time and direction, the peak elevation and the LOS time and direction.
    4. 
        
  4. A rooftop or open ground with a clear view toward the AOS and LOS directions.
    5. 
      

    


    

      
Up to 10 minDuring the pass

      
    
        
  1. Select U1 (or V1), open the squelch, and point the antenna low toward the AOS direction.
    2. 
        
  2. A short buzzing “brrrap” means packets are flowing. Your app will start showing other stations with RS0ISS* in their path.
    3. 
        
  3. Wait about a minute before the next one, and send in gaps between other stations.
    4. 
        
  4. Doppler changes slowly near the horizon and fastest around TCA. The explorer above shows when to switch.
    5. 
        
  5. That is your proof the ISS repeated you, with or without the internet.
    6. 
        
  6. Signals are strongest at the top of the pass: a good moment for a message to a friend in another city.
    7. 
        
  7. Once it is below your horizon, nothing you send can reach it.
    8.

  1. Look for RS0ISS* in the path. An IGate’s qAR or qAO and callsign follow it.
    2. 
        
  2. Both list stations heard through the ISS.
    3. 
        
  3. It tells the next person the digipeater is alive and on which frequency.
    4. 
        
  4. They are your log, and handy if you want a QSL card.
    5. 
        
  5. Success often comes on the second or third pass.

Your packet, piece by piece

    
Everything before the first colon is the header: who sent it, a generic “to” field, and the path. Everything after it is the data. Your app builds this for you; knowing the pieces makes the raw text on aprs.fi readable.

    
VU2ABC-7>APRS,ARISS:=1258.30N/07735.68E-Hi from Bengaluru via ISS

    

      
VU2ABC-7
Source: your callsign and SSID.

      
APRS
Destination: a generic label; apps often put their own short code here.

      
ARISS
Path: asks the ISS to repeat the packet.

      
=
Position report from a station that can receive messages. (! is the same without messaging.)

      
1258.30N
Latitude 12° 58.30′ N, in degrees and minutes.

      
/ and -
Symbol table and symbol: /- draws a house on the map.

      
07735.68E
Longitude 077° 35.68′ E.

      
Hi from…
Your comment. Keep it short.

      Using a classic hardware TNC (from the 2008 tutorial)
      

        
In command mode, set your callsign and path, turn the automatic beacon off, then switch to converse mode. Each line you type and send with Enter goes out as one packet.


MYCALL VU2ABC-7
UNPROTO APRS VIA ARISS
MONITOR ON
CONVERS
=1258.30N/07735.68E-Hi from Bengaluru via ISS

        
The 2008 tutorial also suggested PASSALL ON, MCON ON, FLOW ON, PACLEN 70, and AXDELAY plus TXDELAY of at least 3 on Kantronics-style TNCs. Command names vary by TNC model.

      

    

  






  

    
Good habits

    
The digipeater is one shared channel for a whole continent at a time. When two stations transmit together, the ISS hears only the stronger one, or neither.

    
    
      
  1. Use ARISS as the only path. WIDE paths make ground digipeaters retransmit too and add congestion.
    2. 
      
  2. Never leave an automatic beacon running through the ISS. Send by hand while you are watching the pass.
    3. 
      
  3. About one packet a minute, and keep it short. Stop once you have made it.
    4. 
      
  4. Listen before you transmit, and send in the gaps between other stations.
    5. 
      
  5. Skip the ISS mailbox (PMS/BBS). Bob Bruninga’s ISS FAQ calls it extremely inefficient: one logon attempt can use a whole pass.
    6. 
      
  6. Leave room for others. Dozens of stations may share one pass.
    7.

Glossary

    

      
AOS / TCA / LOS
Rise, highest point and set of a pass.

      
APRS
Automatic Packet Reporting System.

      
AX.25
The packet format APRS uses on air.

      
TNC
Terminal node controller: the packet modem, in hardware or software.

      
Digipeater
Digital repeater: stores, then forwards packets on the same frequency.

      
IGate
A station that copies heard packets to the APRS internet system (APRS-IS). One that listens to satellites is sometimes called a SatGate.

      
Path
Which digipeaters may repeat a packet. For the ISS: ARISS.

      
SSID
The -7 or -9 after a callsign, so one licensee can run several stations.

      
Doppler
Frequency shift caused by the ISS’s motion toward or away from you.

      
TLE
Two-line elements: the orbital data tracking apps use.

      
Grid square
Maidenhead locator such as MK82 (Bengaluru) or ML88 (Delhi).
